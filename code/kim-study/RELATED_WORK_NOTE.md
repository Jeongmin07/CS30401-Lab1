# TCP Reordering Guard and Prior Work

Independent study by kim-seongchan with GPT assistance, October 10, 2026. The research question concerns selective reordering tolerance in the course ns-3.48 CUBIC/SACK/PRR stack. Its defensible contribution is a causal reproduction and an evaluation of activation conditions and failure costs. Reordering tolerance, delayed loss detection and recovery of small application-limited flights already have substantial prior art. This revision incorporates the completed [52-case phase-two study](PHASE2_NOTE.md) and its [results table](phase2_results.csv), alongside the initial [23-case study](EXPERIMENT_NOTE.md). These are deterministic declared cases, not independent seed replicates; teammates' results are excluded.

## What prior work establishes

**LEO route changes can cause packet overtaking.** Kassing et al. demonstrate this in Hypatia's packet-level NewReno experiments: a shorter path lets later packets arrive first and produces unnecessary congestion-window reduction. Stock et al. investigate routing stability and the delay/stability tradeoff. Their artCP transport evaluation models window evolution from RTT and reordering events rather than individual packets, and assumes an always-backlogged sender and unlimited receiver capacity. These works motivate checking the mechanism in the supplied CUBIC stack; neither validates our guard. [Hypatia, Sec. 4.2 and Fig. 4, ACM IMC 2020](https://bdebopam.github.io/papers/imc2020-hypatia.pdf); [Stability-first, Secs. II-B and IV-A, arXiv version 1, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.07460v1).

**Raising the packet threshold is established, including its ACK-starvation cost.** Experimental RFC 4653 specifies TCP-NCR, which delays recovery using a connection-state-dependent threshold and Extended Limited Transmit. Its example of a ten-segment window with one loss explains why threshold ten cannot be reached from the remaining nine segments. It also adapts the threshold when new application data cannot be sent. A scheduled fixed threshold alone is therefore not an implementation of TCP-NCR. [RFC 4653, Secs. 2, 3 and 5, August 2006](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4653.html).

**Time-based loss detection is the relevant modern alternative.** Standards-track RFC 8985 describes RACK, which uses transmission timestamps and ACK/SACK evidence with a bounded reordering allowance; a reordering timer allows progress without another ACK. TLP sends a probe to obtain missing feedback. The RFC expressly discusses application-limited flights and the limitations of packet-count thresholds. Our course stack lacks an implemented RACK-TLP comparison, so its measured failures cannot establish a defect or performance advantage relative to present Linux TCP. [RFC 8985, Secs. 3.1–3.3 and the RACK/TLP algorithms, February 2021](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8985.html).

**Very small flights are a separate known limitation.** Experimental RFC 5827 explains why fewer than four outstanding segments may not supply the three duplicate ACKs required by standard fast retransmit. Early Retransmit conditionally lowers the threshold when no new data can be sent, with an explicit reordering-resilience tradeoff. [RFC 5827, Secs. 1, 3 and 4.2, April 2010](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5827.html).

## Completed threshold and finite-size comparisons

The completed 10-Mbit/s LEO cases already show identical receiver goodput for event thresholds 8, 20 and 32. This is one supplied path and controller configuration, not a threshold optimum. The short-flow K20 counterexample sends 20 MSS of data and drops the **first** data segment; its case name contains “tail,” but it is not a last-packet-loss experiment. See the independent [experiment note](EXPERIMENT_NOTE.md).

The new 20-MSS first-segment-loss control now measures K8 directly. Baseline completes at **0.251509920 s**, K20 at **1.150124001 s**, and K8 at **0.350482400 s**. K8 avoids the K20 timeout/`CA_LOSS` path but still adds **98.97248 ms** relative to baseline; it is a partial repair of this measured prototype failure. The earlier bulk LEO K8 run retains the tested first-event benefit. There is only one finite-flow K8 case, so this evidence does not establish K8's behavior across the complete size or loss-position matrix. See [phase-two measurements](PHASE2_NOTE.md) and the [optional K8 figure](figures/phase2-with-k8/finite_size_first_loss.pdf).

The implemented refinement instead bypasses the guard before connection establishment when the application's known nonzero total size satisfies `ceil(MaxBytes / SegmentSize) ≤ 21`. It keeps threshold 3 throughout an exempt transfer. This is an upfront size policy, not a dynamic bound on outstanding flight, remaining bytes or available ACK evidence. For 20-MSS first loss, the exemption restores baseline **0.251509920 s**; for middle loss it restores baseline **0.350711200 s**, compared with K20's **1.251165120 s**. It also restores baseline timing for the tested 4-, 8- and 21-MSS first-loss cases. Exactly 21 MSS activates the exemption; 22 MSS does not and retains the observed penalty: protected **0.450716800 s** versus baseline **0.350597120 s**. The cutoff is an exploratory diagnostic choice, not a derived optimum.

First- and last-segment loss answer different questions. At 3 MSS with first loss, baseline, K20 and exemption all enter `CA_LOSS` and complete at **1.150124001 s**. With last-segment loss, all three modes time out: **1.351284160 s** at 20 MSS and **1.552210560 s** at 80 MSS. The exemption preserves baseline behavior; it does not manufacture feedback after the last packet or remove an existing baseline timeout. There is no completed 2-MSS or tiny-flow K8 comparison in this matrix, and no claim about those unmeasured combinations is warranted.

The exemption leaves bulk LEO protection active because the configured transfer is larger than its cutoff. The first-event application-goodput benefit remains **1.4432%**, while whole-run improvement remains only **0.02256%**. In the separate 80-MSS delay-step control, where the exemption also does not apply, baseline completes at **0.611527200 s** and both protected variants at **0.430944960 s**. Matched stable-delay controls finish at **0.452206240 s** in all modes. These six controls have zero observed tagged drops, and the step's eight baseline retransmissions are causally audited in the [phase-two note](PHASE2_NOTE.md). This is a synthetic control rather than a general LEO throughput result. The tested 20-MSS step has no harmful recovery, so it does not establish that exempt small transfers never benefit from reordering tolerance.

The receiver's number of arriving later segments limits available loss evidence, but duplicate-ACK count is not the whole rule in this SACK stack. The configured threshold also affects SACK scoreboard loss inference. Use the actual ACK/SACK trace and segment boundaries; do not derive an optimal K from the five observed out-of-order arrivals alone.

A scheduled guard end is also not a guaranteed loss-repair deadline. In the inspected source, `TcpSocketBase::SetRetxThresh` assigns the socket threshold and transmit-buffer duplicate-ACK threshold; it does not itself retransmit or run an ACK handler. Deferring restoration during an unsafe state can extend protection. Therefore a timer that merely restores K cannot promise prompt recovery when no further ACK arrives. A stronger fallback needs explicit loss reevaluation or a probe, and would need independent implementation and validation. This is a source-based inference, not a completed alternative protocol.

## Contribution framing for the report

A suitable framing is **route-informed reordering tolerance and its loss-recovery tradeoffs in a supplied LEO TCP model**. The report can support three contributions:

1. Establish one concrete CUBIC/SACK/PRR episode using transmission identity, receiver acceptance, ACK/SACK feedback, recovery state and drop coverage; reproduce the delayed original rather than infer loss from cwnd alone.
2. Evaluate temporary activation against both unchanged TCP and a continuous threshold, including late notices and negative queue conditions. Identify the difference between a small event-local benefit and whole-run performance.
3. Demonstrate the genuine-loss failure envelope: recovery delay, application completion, insufficient feedback and actual restoration time. Quantify K8's partial repair and the known-size exemption's preservation of baseline recovery, including the 21/22-MSS boundary and genuine last-segment-loss limits.

Avoid claims of inventing reordering robustness, fully implementing NCR or RACK, universal throughput improvement, or fixing deployed Starlink/Linux. The activation schedule uses simulation routing knowledge. Operator notifications or routing prediction, timing errors and prediction availability remain deployment requirements. A successful scheduled guard in this model does not demonstrate that an ordinary endpoint has that information. The size exemption additionally needs the application's total-size information; an ordinary open-ended stream may not provide it. Exemption gives up increased reordering tolerance for those transfers rather than improving their loss detector.

The first-loss size matrix has now been completed for baseline, K20 and exemption at **3, 4, 8, 20, 21, 22 and 80 MSS**, with a separate K8 point at 20 MSS. The 52 cases also include first/middle/last loss controls, stable no-loss controls, finite delay decreases, bulk LEO preservation and six nonzero protection-window offsets. Offset zero uses the independently simulated nominal bulk control. These offsets shift both endpoints of the protection windows; they do not simulate notice-message arrival times. The [phase-two figures and source hashes](figures/phase2/figure_sources.json) preserve actual settings and measurement windows.

The remaining comparisons are broader K8 and tiny-flight coverage, an independently implemented RACK-TLP or Early Retransmit alternative, and a policy based on actual remaining feedback rather than known total size. Any new early-retransmit, loss-reevaluation timer or probe rule needs tiny reordered-flight controls because faster genuine-loss recovery can increase unnecessary retransmission. These alternatives remain proposed work, not completed protocol comparisons. The present contribution is the measured activation/size tradeoff and its counterexamples, with established loss-recovery prior art acknowledged.
